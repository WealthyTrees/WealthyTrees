Module 2: Valuation

Two shops each earn ₹10 lakh a year. One is for sale at ₹1 crore, the other at ₹3 crore. Is the second one a worse deal? Not automatically. It depends on how fast it grows, how safe it is, and what you really get for the price. Valuation is the habit of asking that question.

How it connects. Module 1 taught you to read a company’s statements. Here you turn those numbers into a price check.

P/E = price divided by earnings per share. Example numbers: 200 / 10 = 20.

1. P/E: price to earnings

What it is. Share price divided by earnings per share (EPS). It tells you how many rupees you pay for each rupee of yearly profit. Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of actual profit. Forward P/E uses estimates, and estimates can be wrong.

Where to find it. The stock screener on the Markets page, and the quote page of the company on NSE or BSE. NSE also publishes P/E, P/B and dividend yield for its indices.

How to read it. A P/E means little alone. Compare it with companies in the same sector and with the company’s own past. A high P/E can mean the market expects growth. A low P/E can mean value, or trouble.

Common trap. Comparing P/E across different sectors, or using it when profit is falling or lifted by a one-off gain. A company with a loss has no meaningful P/E.

2. P/B: price to book

What it is. Share price divided by book value per share. Book value is net worth (equity) divided by the number of shares.

How to read it. It works best for businesses built on assets, such as banks and other financial companies. Pair it with return on equity (ROE): a company that earns a higher ROE can reasonably trade at a higher P/B.

Common trap. Book value ignores brands, software and people, so P/B is a poor guide for asset-light businesses.

3. EV/EBITDA

What it is. Enterprise value (EV) is market cap plus net debt (borrowings minus cash). EBITDA is profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating earnings. EV/EBITDA prices the whole business, so companies with different debt levels can be compared fairly.

Where to find it. Build it yourself from the results: operating profit plus depreciation gives a quick EBITDA, and the balance sheet gives borrowings and cash.

Enterprise value adds net debt to market cap, so companies with different debt can be compared.

Common trap. EBITDA ignores capital spending and interest. A business that must keep buying equipment looks cheaper on EV/EBITDA than it really is. It is also not meaningful for banks.

4. A simple DCF

The idea. A business is worth the cash it will produce in future, brought back to today’s value. Four steps: estimate free cash flow for a few years, choose a discount rate (the return you demand), estimate a value for the years beyond (terminal value), then add up the discounted amounts.

Why discount. Money in hand today can earn a return, so cash promised later is worth less now. At 10% a year, ₹100 three years away is worth about ₹75 today. At 12% it is worth about ₹71.

A rupee later is worth less today. At 10% a year, ₹100 three years away is worth about ₹75 now.

Common trap. A DCF is only as good as its assumptions. A small change in growth or the discount rate moves the answer a lot, so work with a range of values, not one number.

5. Putting it together

  • Use two or three tools, not one.
  • Compare with peers in the same sector and with the company’s own history.
  • Ask what growth the current price is already assuming.
  • Check the numbers behind the ratio: profit, debt and cash flow from Module 1.

Valuation tells you what a price implies, not what to do. Takeaway: a good company can be a poor buy at a high price, and a low price can be low for a reason.
