Module 1: Financial statements

Two shops both report a profit of ₹10 lakh. One was paid in cash the same day. The other is still waiting for money from customers who may never pay. Same profit, very different business. The three statements help you tell them apart.

How it connects. In Beginner you met the three statements. Here you learn how they link, and how to spot a story that does not add up.

The three statements are linked. Profit feeds equity; cash flow moves the cash balance.

1. The profit and loss, one step further

What it is. The story of a period, from revenue at the top to net profit at the bottom. In between sit operating profit (profit from the core business), other income (interest, gains and so on), interest and tax.

Where to find it. On NSE or BSE, search the company and open its financial results. Listed companies publish quarterly results within 45 days of the quarter end and full-year results within 60 days of the financial year end (SEBI listing rules, checked Oct 2026). The Indian financial year runs 1 April to 31 March, so Q1 is April to June.

How to read it. Work down the ladder. Is operating profit growing? How much of net profit comes from other income? Check the unit at the top of the table (crore or lakh) and whether you are looking at standalone (the company alone) or consolidated (the company and its subsidiaries together).

Common trap. A rising net profit that comes mostly from other income or a one-off gain says little about the core business.

From revenue to net profit. Example numbers, in rupees crore.

2. The balance sheet, beyond the basics

What it is. A snapshot on one date: what the company owns, what it owes, and what is left for shareholders (net worth or equity).

How to read it. Compare borrowings with cash. Borrowings minus cash is a quick measure of net debt. Then look at the money tied up in running the business: receivables (what customers owe), inventory (stock), and payables (what the company owes suppliers).

Common trap. Receivables growing much faster than revenue. A simple check is receivable days: receivables divided by yearly revenue, times 365. If the number keeps rising, customers are paying more slowly.

3. The cash flow statement

What it is. The real cash that came in and went out, in three parts: operations, investing (buying or selling equipment and investments) and financing (loans, share issues, dividends).

How to read it. Free cash flow is cash from operations minus capital spending (capex). It shows what is left after the business pays for its own equipment and expansion.

Common trap. One strong year of cash from operations can come from delaying payments to suppliers. Look at three to five years, not one.

Profit is not cash. Example numbers: profit 17, but only 6 came in as cash from operations.

4. How the three statements connect

The idea. Profit stays in the business as equity unless it is paid out as dividends. The cash flow statement explains how the cash balance on the balance sheet changed: closing cash equals opening cash plus the net cash flow of the year. When the statements disagree, there is a question to ask.

5. A five-minute quality check

  • Cash from operations compared with net profit over three years: close to or above 1 times, or far below?
  • Receivables growing faster than revenue?
  • What share of net profit is other income?
  • Are borrowings rising while cash is not?
  • Any exceptional or one-off items in the ladder?

A flag is a question to research, not a verdict. Takeaway: profit tells the story, cash flow checks it.
