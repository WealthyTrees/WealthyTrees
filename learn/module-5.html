Module 5: From RBI to your portfolio

Big news such as a rate decision or an oil price jump reaches your money through a chain of steps. Learn the chain and headlines become easier to read. All numbers and flows below are simplified and made up to teach the idea.

How it connects. Charts show what prices did. This module looks at the news that helps explain why.

1. What the RBI does

What it is. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the country's central bank. Its Monetary Policy Committee meets several times a year and sets the repo rate, the rate at which RBI lends short-term to banks. It also aims to keep inflation in check.

The repo rate is the rate at which RBI lends short-term money to banks.

Where to find it. Policy decisions and statements are published on the RBI website, and every major news outlet reports them the same day.

How to read it. Read the decision in three parts: did the rate change, what was the stance (the RBI's signal about direction), and what did it say about inflation and growth.

Common trap. The headline number is only part of it. The tone of the statement can move markets more than the rate itself.

Try it. Open the latest RBI policy press release. Write down the repo rate, the stance and one line on inflation.

2. From interest rates to markets

What it is. When the repo rate falls, banks can lend more cheaply, so loans and EMIs tend to get cheaper. When it rises, borrowing gets costlier. This changes what households spend and what companies pay on their debt.

Rate-sensitive areas such as banks, housing and autos tend to react first.

Where to find it. Look at your bank's loan and fixed deposit rates, and at the news on bond yields. Both move after policy decisions.

How to read it. Ask who borrows heavily and who earns from interest. Banks, housing, autos and highly indebted companies are rate-sensitive. Savers see fixed deposit rates move the opposite way to borrowers' loan rates.

Common trap. Markets look ahead. If a cut was expected, share prices may already have moved, so a cut can bring little or even a fall.

Try it. Pick one rate-sensitive company. Check its debt to equity (Module 3) and write one line on how a rate rise could hurt it.

3. Crude oil and India

What it is. India imports most of the crude oil it uses. When oil gets dearer, the country pays more for imports, and fuel and freight costs rise across the economy.

India imports most of its crude, so oil price moves matter here. Made-up flow, simplified.

Where to find it. Brent crude prices appear on commodity and market news pages. Indian crude futures trade on MCX.

How to read it. Rising oil can squeeze companies that use fuel as a big cost, such as airlines and transport. It can help oil producers. Higher fuel costs also feed inflation, which the RBI watches.

Common trap. Do not assume oil up means every share down. Effects differ by company, and a stronger rupee or a quick fall in oil can reverse the story.

Try it. Pick one company hurt by oil and one that may gain. Write one line on why they differ.

4. Connecting the dots

What it is. Good readers turn news into three questions: who is hit, how are they hit (costs, demand or funding), and how big and how soon, or was it already expected?

A headline is the start of your questions, not the end.

How to read it. Write the chain on paper: event, then first effect, then second effect, then the company. If you cannot finish the chain, you do not yet understand the news.

Common trap. One headline rarely tells the whole story, and reacting to every headline is a habit that tends to cost money. Slow down and finish the chain first.

Try it. Take today's biggest market headline. Answer the three questions in three lines.
