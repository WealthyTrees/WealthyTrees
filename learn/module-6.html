Module 6: Risk and behaviour

Knowing the numbers is half the job. The other half is you. This last module covers what risk really means, how to spread it, and the habits that make smart people lose money. All examples are made up to teach the idea.

1. Risk and reward

What it is. Risk is the chance that things turn out worse than you hoped, including losing money. Reward is the possible gain. The two travel together: a chance of a bigger gain usually means a chance of a bigger loss.

Risk is the chance that things turn out worse than you hoped, including losing money.

Where to find it. A chart (Module 4) shows how much a share's price swung in the past. Ratios such as debt to equity (Module 3) show how much strain a company carries.

How to read it. Bigger swings mean higher risk. Ask two questions: how much could I lose, and could I sit through it calmly?

Common trap. A calm past does not make a share safe. And anyone promising high returns with no risk is a warning sign, not a bargain.

Try it. Compare the 1-year charts of two shares. Which one swung more? Write how you would feel if you owned it during its worst week.

2. Spreading risk

What it is. Spreading money across different companies, sectors and kinds of assets, so one problem cannot sink everything. This is often called diversification.

Spreading helps because one company's problem hurts only part of your money. Made-up example.

Where to find it. Mutual funds and index funds hold many shares in one product. Fund fact sheets list their holdings and sectors.

How to read it. Count sectors, not just companies. Five banks are five companies but one sector, so a banking problem hits all of them.

Common trap. Spreading reduces risk but never removes it. In a market-wide fall, most things drop together. And owning many things you do not understand is not spreading, it is confusion.

Try it. List any set of shares you are curious about and tag each with its sector. Is most of it in one sector?

3. Behaviour traps

What it is. Markets move on numbers and on emotion. Common traps: greed (chasing what is rising), fear (selling in panic), herd thinking (doing what the crowd does), overconfidence, and hanging on to losers just to break even.

Emotions make the same loop feel new every time. Seeing it is the first defence.

Where to find it. In your own notes, in social media tips, and in headline frenzies. Be careful with tips from strangers online, and check whether the person is SEBI-registered.

How to read it. Write your reason before you act, and make your rules in a calm moment. If you still want to act after waiting a day, you have given it fair thought.

Common trap. “Everyone is buying” feels like proof, but it is not. By the time a crowd is excited, much of the move has often already happened.

Try it. Recall a time you followed a crowd, in money or elsewhere. Write what you felt and what you would do differently.
